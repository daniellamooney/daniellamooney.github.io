A body of work. Bodies at work. A body, worked.
Contemplations on Close the Hole by Daniella Mooney
nicola van Straaten

I come home from the late shift. The house is dark and quiet but her light is still on, working quietly. I don't know what she does in there but she's busy. One afternoon I'm finally invited in and there they are - spread out on the floor in the too dark room, about twenty paintings.

On meeting them, I meet a wall, a floor, a cave, a nightmare, a dream, hidden figures and faces embedded in shape and stone. From too real to surreal, I meet a heavy recognition of something language insists on failing at. I meet a sound that is both terrible silence and terrible wailing. I meet a roar of machinery and an agonizing grunt of humanity. I meet a quiet that hums, like a haunting. I meet a face of time, made manifest. It humbles me.

We kneel before the floored wall of work, leaning over in awe as she talks us through the dark labyrinth of reckoning, of grief, of the kind of care that is no performance. 'Humble' is a word that comes from the Latin humilis, which literally means "on the ground" and comes from the Proto-Indo-European root *dhghem, which means "earth". It takes a humble person, a person deeply on and in and of the ground to produce such a densely rigorous collection of paintings.

I've been living with Daniella for six years now. To live with an artist is to also live with their work, which is to say - to live with an artist is to also live with a living, breathing entity that has weight and whims and rhythms of its own. Living with Daniella's work is like living with a solitary concentrated mass of energy that mostly goes unnoticed until one day it reveals itself, fills a room and you're weeping before it.

Daniella sometimes refers to herself as a landscape painter with a kind of chuckle at the antiquated title of her job. But in Close the Hole, which she regards as one work, she has undone something key to landscape painting as a practice. She has pushed and pulled and wrestled with both the practice and idea of representing a landscape, critically morphing it into something that aligns with her concerns and values. Here, she enters land, goes beneath and into it. Evading the inevitable framing that comes with portraying a landscape, she tugs on something deeper and asks us to join her in shifting our perspective of land as something we are not on, but in and in relation with.

To me, this work is a relief of an invitation. It invites me to mourn and it reminds me of the imperative of staying with, being with and of looking. Looking closer, deeper, beneath and beyond. Of looking until you see the thing before you become a mirror, sharing minerals, chemicals, particles. Perhaps it's the evocations of burial, the commentaries on time or the resonances of abused bodies and lands that colonial modernity insists on. Whatever it is, standing before this work feels like standing before an incredibly solid ghost that wordlessly provides space for you to grieve and simply be with the memorial that is stone, the slow archivist that is earth and especially, to be with the question of our relationship with, to and of land.
